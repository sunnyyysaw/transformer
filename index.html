Part 01

Why Did We Need Something New?

Before the Transformer, machines translated languages using a model called an RNN (Recurrent Neural Network). Imagine reading a book but only being allowed to remember the last sentence — that's basically what RNNs did. They processed words one-by-one, left to right, always forgetting the distant past.

🔍 Analogy

Think of it like a game of telephone: you whisper a message across 20 people. By the time it reaches the end, the original meaning is often garbled. RNNs had the same problem with long sentences.

RNNs had two big problems:

Fig 1.1 — RNN processes words sequentially; Transformer processes all words in parallel, letting every word attend to every other

🐢 RNN's Problems

Processes words one at a time — slow to train. Forgets information from far-back in the sentence (vanishing gradient).

⚡ Transformer's Solution

Processes all words simultaneously. Every word can directly attend to every other — no forgetting, fully parallel.

In 2017, a group of Google researchers published a paper called "Attention Is All You Need" and introduced the Transformer. It changed everything.

Part 02

The Big Picture: A Black Box That Translates

Let's start at the highest level. A Transformer takes in a sequence (like an English sentence) and produces another sequence (like a French translation). Think of it as a very smart machine with two main parts inside:

Fig 2.1 — The Transformer at the highest level: Encoder reads & understands, Decoder writes & generates

Both the encoder and decoder are stacked in layers — the original paper uses 6 layers of each. Each layer refines the understanding a little more, like an editor reviewing a draft multiple times.

Part 03

Step 1: Turning Words Into Numbers

Computers don't understand words — they understand numbers. So the very first thing the Transformer does is convert each word into a vector (a list of 512 numbers).

🔍 Analogy

Think of a word embedding like a GPS coordinate for meaning. "King" and "Queen" will be plotted near each other in 512-dimensional space. "Apple" and "Mango" will be close together, far from "Rocket." The numbers capture semantic meaning.

Fig 3.1 — Each word is converted into a vector of 512 numbers (shown as coloured bars). Similar words will have similar vectors.

But there's a problem: these embeddings have no sense of order. "The cat sat" and "Sat cat the" would produce the same embeddings! We need to bake in position information.

Positional Encoding: Telling Words Where They Stand

The Transformer adds a special positional encoding vector to each word embedding. This is a unique fingerprint for each position (1st word, 2nd word, 3rd word...) built from sine and cosine waves.

Fig 3.2 — Word embedding + Positional encoding = a vector rich with both meaning and position information

Part 04 — The Heart of the Transformer

Self-Attention: Every Word Talks to Every Other Word

This is the most important concept. Self-attention lets each word look at every other word in the sentence and ask: "How relevant are you to understanding me?"

🔍 The "It" Problem

Consider: "The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired."

What does "it" refer to? The street or the animal? A human knows instantly — "it" = animal. Self-attention teaches the model to make this connection by letting "it" look at all other words and figure out that "animal" is the most relevant one.

Here's how self-attention works. For each word, we create 3 vectors:

Fig 4.1 — Each word generates 3 vectors (Q, K, V) by multiplying with learned weight matrices. These are used to compute attention scores.

The Library Analogy: Q, K, V Explained

📚 Library Search Analogy

Imagine you walk into a library. You have a Query (your search question), the library has books with Keys (titles/tags on the spine), and each book has Values (actual content inside).

You match your Query against all the Keys, find the most relevant ones, and read proportionally more from those books. That's exactly what attention does — every word "searches" among all other words and reads more from the most relevant ones.

The 6 Steps of Self-Attention

1

Create Q, K, V vectors

Multiply each word's embedding by three weight matrices (WQ, WK, WV) to produce a Query, Key, and Value vector for every word (64 dimensions each).

2

Calculate attention scores

For each word, take the dot product of its Query with every other word's Key. Higher score = more relevance. Think: "How much should word X pay attention to word Y?"

3

Scale down by √64

Divide scores by √64 = 8. This prevents the dot products from getting huge, which would make gradients unstable during training.

4

Apply Softmax

Softmax converts all scores into probabilities (positive, summing to 1). The most relevant words get scores close to 1, irrelevant ones close to 0.

5

Multiply by Value vectors

Multiply each word's Value vector by its softmax score. High-score words contribute more; low-score words barely contribute (nearly zeroed out).

6

Sum everything up

Add all the weighted Value vectors. The result is a single rich vector for each word — it now contains not just the word's own meaning, but a blend of context from all relevant words.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q · Kᵀ / √d_k ) · V

Fig 4.2 — When encoding "it", the model's attention focuses heavily on "animal" and "tired" — correctly resolving the ambiguity

Part 05

Multi-Head Attention: Multiple Perspectives at Once

One attention mechanism gives one "view" of relationships. But language is rich — a word can relate to others for many reasons (grammar, coreference, proximity, meaning). So the Transformer runs 8 attention heads in parallel, each learning a different type of relationship.

🔍 Analogy

Imagine 8 different editors reading the same manuscript simultaneously — one focuses on grammar, one on theme, one on character relationships, one on tone. Each editor produces their own set of notes, then they all combine notes for the final draft. That's multi-head attention.

Fig 5.1 — 8 attention heads run in parallel, each learning different relationships. Their outputs are concatenated and projected into a single 512-d vector.

Each head independently runs the full Q/K/V calculation with its own set of weight matrices. After all heads run, their outputs (8 × 64-d = 512-d) are concatenated and multiplied by one final weight matrix W_O to give the final 512-d output.

Part 06

Inside One Encoder Layer

Each encoder layer has two sub-layers. After passing through both, the output moves up to the next encoder layer.

Fig 6.1 — One encoder layer: self-attention followed by feed-forward. Each sub-layer has a residual (skip) connection and layer normalization.

Why Residual Connections?

The residual connection (skip connection) adds the original input back to the output of each sub-layer. This solves the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks — gradients can flow directly through the shortcut path during backpropagation, making training of 6+ layers feasible.

What Does the Feed-Forward Layer Do?

After attention has mixed information across words, the feed-forward network applies the same transformation to each word independently. Think of it as each word doing its own private "thinking" — processing what it learned from the attention step before passing it up.

Part 07

The Decoder: Generating the Output

While the encoder reads the whole input at once, the decoder generates output one word at a time. It has three sub-layers — one extra compared to the encoder.

Fig 7.1 — The decoder has 3 sub-layers. Cross-attention is the key new addition — it "reads" the encoded source and uses it to guide generation.

The cross-attention layer is what connects encoder and decoder. The decoder uses its own current state as the Query, and the encoder's full output as the Keys and Values. This is how the decoder always knows "what the input sentence said" while generating each output word.

Masking: No Cheating!

During training, the decoder processes all output words at once for efficiency. But to prevent it from "seeing the answer," future positions are masked (set to −∞ before softmax, making their attention scores essentially zero). This forces the model to predict each word using only the past.

Part 08

From Numbers Back to Words

The decoder's final vector still needs to become an actual word. This happens in two steps:

Fig 8.1 — Decoder vector → Linear projection → Softmax probabilities → Pick highest probability word

The chosen word is fed back into the decoder as input for the next step. This autoregressive generation continues until the model outputs a special <END> token.

Part 11

Why This Changed Everything

🚀 Parallelisation

Because all words are processed simultaneously (not sequentially), Transformers can be trained on massive datasets using GPUs/TPUs at unprecedented speed.

🧠 Long-Range Dependencies

Any word can directly attend to any other word regardless of distance. No more forgetting — the model captures relationships across entire documents.

📐 Scalability

The architecture scales beautifully. Add more layers, more heads, bigger embeddings — and performance keeps improving (GPT-4, Gemini, Claude all follow this pattern).

🌍 Beyond Translation

Though designed for translation, the Transformer became the backbone of GPT, BERT, T5, and every modern LLM — powering chatbots, code assistants, image generation, and more.

The key insight: "Attention is All You Need." You don't need recurrence or convolutions. A simple attention mechanism, scaled up and stacked, is sufficient to learn remarkably complex representations of language.
